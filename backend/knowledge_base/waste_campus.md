# Waste Management — Campus

## Overview
Effective waste management on campus reduces environmental impact, supports recycling and circular economy principles, and maintains a clean, healthy environment for students, staff, and visitors. Campus waste includes general refuse, recyclables, food waste, e-waste, hazardous laboratory waste, and construction debris.

---

## Waste Segregation on Campus

### Why Segregation Matters
Mixing different types of waste makes recycling impossible and increases the amount sent to landfill. Segregated waste enables recyclables to be recovered, organic waste to be composted, and hazardous materials to be safely disposed of.

### Standard Waste Categories and Bin Colours
Different institutions use different colour codes; the following is a widely used scheme in Indian institutions:
- **Blue bin** — Dry recyclable waste: paper, cardboard, plastic bottles, metal cans, glass bottles.
- **Green bin** — Wet/organic waste: food scraps, vegetable peels, biodegradable material.
- **Red bin** — Hazardous and biomedical waste: broken glass, sharps (syringes, needles), chemical waste containers.
- **Black / Grey bin** — Reject/residual waste: items that cannot be recycled or composted.

### How to Segregate Correctly
- Rinse food containers before placing them in the dry waste bin.
- Do not mix wet and dry waste — contamination renders recyclables unusable.
- Flatten cardboard boxes and fold paper to save space in bins.
- Remove lids and caps from bottles; sort separately if bins are provided.
- Never put electronic items in general waste bins.

## Bin Overflow and Collection Issues

### Overflowing Bins
Overflowing bins attract pests, create odour problems, and are a health hazard.
- Report overflowing bins to the Campus Housekeeping or Facilities team.
- Provide the exact location (building, floor, zone).
- If bins are routinely overflowing, the collection schedule or bin capacity may need to be reviewed by campus management.

### Collection Schedule
- Most campuses have daily or twice-daily collection from high-usage areas (cafeterias, common rooms).
- Report irregular or missed collections to the Campus Housekeeping team.

## Food Waste on Campus

### Scale of the Problem
Food waste accounts for a significant proportion of campus organic waste, particularly from cafeterias, events, and residential dining halls.

### Practical Reduction Measures
- Take only what you can eat in cafeterias; avoid over-serving.
- Composting programmes convert food waste into useful compost for campus gardens.
- Leftover food from events can be donated to on-campus food banks or community organisations if handled safely.
- Avoid single-use packaging for food and beverages; use reusable containers.

### Composting on Campus
- Many campuses operate centralised composting facilities that convert organic waste into compost used for campus landscaping.
- Students and staff can contribute by correctly depositing food waste in designated green bins.
- Community composting workshops raise awareness and build skills.

## Electronic Waste (E-Waste) on Campus

### What Counts as E-Waste
Computers, laptops, monitors, printers, mobile phones, batteries, chargers, laboratory instruments, and any other electronic device at end of life.

### Why E-Waste Is Hazardous
E-waste contains toxic materials including lead, mercury, cadmium, and brominated flame retardants. Disposing of e-waste in general bins is illegal in many countries and causes soil and water contamination.

### Proper E-Waste Disposal on Campus
1. Contact the Campus IT Department or Facilities team for scheduled e-waste collection drives.
2. Do not leave e-waste in general waste bins or corridors.
3. Functional older equipment can be donated to on-campus or community reuse programmes.
4. Batteries (laptop, AA, AAA, phone) should be deposited in designated battery collection boxes.

## Hazardous and Laboratory Waste

### Types of Hazardous Campus Waste
- Chemical reagents, solvents, and acids from laboratories
- Biological waste (cultures, contaminated materials)
- Radioactive materials (if applicable)
- Mercury-containing equipment (thermometers, fluorescent lamps)

### Safe Disposal
- All hazardous waste must follow the campus laboratory safety protocol.
- Contact the Campus Laboratory Safety Officer for disposal procedures specific to each chemical.
- Never pour chemical waste down drains — this contaminates water systems.
- Segregate biological waste in autoclave bags and follow biosafety procedures.

---

## Single-Use Plastics on Campus

### Reducing Single-Use Plastic
Many campuses have introduced or are working towards banning single-use plastics (cups, straws, cutlery, carrier bags).
- Carry a reusable water bottle and coffee cup.
- Refuse unnecessary plastic bags; use cloth or jute bags.
- Support campus initiatives to switch to compostable or reusable alternatives.
- Advocate for water refill stations across campus to reduce plastic bottle use.

---

## Litter and Illegal Dumping on Campus

- Report litter hotspots or fly-tipping to Campus Housekeeping or Security.
- Participate in campus clean-up events.
- Use the nearest available bin rather than leaving waste on floors or outdoor spaces.

---

## Key Contacts (Campus)
- **Campus Housekeeping / Sanitation Team** — bin overflow, collection schedules, general litter.
- **Campus Facilities / Maintenance** — infrastructure issues (broken bins, compactors).
- **Campus Laboratory Safety Officer** — hazardous and chemical waste disposal.
- **Campus IT Department** — e-waste collection and equipment disposal.
- **Campus Environmental Officer** — sustainability initiatives, composting programmes, and plastic reduction campaigns.
